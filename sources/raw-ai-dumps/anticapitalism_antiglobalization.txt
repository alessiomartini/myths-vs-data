
Anti-capitalist and anti-globalization critiques target the core mechanics of modern market economies and international integration, while counter-arguments defend these systems through growth, poverty reduction, and institutional reform. [1, 2, 3]
Anti-Capitalist Critiques & Counter-Arguments
•	Exploitation and Inequality:
	◦	Critique: Capitalism extracts surplus value from workers, concentrates wealth in the hands of a small owner class, and deepens economic disparity.
	◦	Counter-Argument: Proponents argue that market competition and productivity growth raise overall living standards, expand consumer access to goods, and incentivize innovation that benefits society as a whole. [1, 2, 3]
•	Instability and Crises:
	◦	Critique: Unfettered markets suffer from recurring boom-and-bust cycles, systemic financial panics, and the "socialization of risk" (such as corporate bailouts).
	◦	Counter-Argument: Defenders point out that modern economies use monetary policy, central banking, and regulatory frameworks to smooth out cycles, and that market failures can be managed without scrapping private property or price signals. [1, 2, 3]
•	Environmental Degradation:
	◦	Critique: The mandate for endless capital accumulation treats finite natural resources as free dumping grounds, driving climate change and biodiversity loss.
	◦	Counter-Argument: Market advocates contend that green capitalism, carbon pricing, and technological innovation provide the fastest path to resource efficiency and renewable energy transitions. [1, 2]

Anti-Globalization Critiques & Counter-Arguments
•	Job Losses and Deindustrialization:
	◦	Critique: Cross-border trade and offshoring depress wages and eliminate manufacturing jobs in developed nations, hollowing out working-class communities.
	◦	Counter-Argument: Economists argue that comparative advantage lowers consumer prices, drives global specialization, and creates new service and tech-sector jobs, even if localized transitions require retraining support. [1, 2, 3]
•	Erosion of National Sovereignty:
	◦	Critique: Global supply chains, multinational corporations, and supranational bodies (like the WTO or IMF) override local democratic decision-making and national economic controls.
	◦	Counter-Argument: Supporters maintain that international rules prevent protectionist trade wars, foster diplomatic cooperation, and spread democratic norms through economic engagement. [1, 2]
•	Asymmetrical Power and Exploitation of the Global South:
	◦	Critique: Globalization often locks developing nations into low-wage resource extraction or sweatshop labor while rich countries impose lopsided trade barriers and agricultural subsidies.
	◦	Counter-Argument: Free-market proponents emphasize that global trade has lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty and that protectionist barriers—not openness—tend to harm developing farmers the most. [1, 2, 3]
