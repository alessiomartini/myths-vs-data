Liberalism is a political and moral philosophy centered on the idea that individual liberty, equal rights, and the rule of law are the fundamental foundations of a just society. [1, 2, 3]
Throughout history, the primary claims and arguments advanced by liberals have evolved, leading to a distinction between classical and modern interpretations. The core arguments used to support the liberal framework span across several key pillars. [1]

1. The Right to Liberty and the Social Contract
•	Inalienable Natural Rights: All individuals possess inherent rights to life, liberty, and property that no government can justly strip away. [1, 2]
•	Consent of the Governed: Political authority is not derived from divine right or bloodlines, but from a "social contract" where citizens agree to be ruled only in exchange for the protection of their basic rights. [1, 2, 3]
•	The Harm Principle: Individual freedom should only be limited to prevent immediate, direct harm to others. [1, 2]
2. Equality and Pluralism
•	Equality Before the Law: Every citizen, regardless of status, identity, or wealth, is subject to the exact same legal standards and entitled to equal protection. [1, 2]
•	The Liberal Compromise: Because reasonable people naturally develop different moral, religious, and personal values, society must create a neutral framework allowing diverse groups to coexist peacefully without imposing one supreme value system. [1]
•	Universal Suffrage: True democratic governance requires expanding voting rights and civic participation to all adult citizens. [1, 2]
3. Institutional Limits on Power
•	Skepticism of Authority: Because power naturally corrupts, government capability must be strictly divided, checked, and limited.
•	Separation of Powers: Mainstream liberal structures require distinct branches of government (executive, legislative, and an independent judiciary) to prevent autocracy.
•	Secularism: True freedom of conscience requires a strict separation of church and state, keeping religious doctrines out of public legislation. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

The Evolution: Classical vs. Modern Claims
While sharing a foundation of individual rights, the philosophy split during the 19th and 20th centuries over the economic role of the state. [1]
Concept	Classical Liberalism (Libertarianism)	Modern Liberalism (Social Liberalism)
Primary Threat	The state and government overreach.	Private economic actors, systemic poverty, and discrimination.
Economic View	Laissez-faire capitalism, free markets, minimal taxation, and unregulated voluntary exchange.	Regulated capitalism with state-guided safety nets and public infrastructure.
Definition of Freedom	Negative liberty: The absolute absence of external obstacles or government coercion.	Positive liberty: The capacity and resources to achieve one's potential (freedom from hunger/disease).
Core Policy Focus	Protecting private property, maintaining free trade, and restricting government size.	Promoting social welfare, civil rights, environmental justice, and equal economic opportunity.
