Marxism is a social, political, and economic theory founded by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels that analyzes how capitalism works and how society evolves. [1, 2]
Core Claims and Concepts
•	Historical Materialism: The belief that material conditions and economic relations (who owns the factories, land, and resources) shape society, politics, and culture—rather than abstract ideas or individual choices. [1, 2]
•	Class Struggle: History is driven by conflict between social classes. Under capitalism, this is primarily the bourgeoisie (those who own the means of production and capital) and the proletariat (the working class who sell their labor to survive). [1, 2, 3]
•	Surplus Value and Exploitation: Workers generate value through their labor, but capitalists pay them only a fraction of that value in wages while keeping the rest as profit (surplus value). [1, 2]
•	Alienation: Capitalism separates workers from what they make, from the act of production itself, from their human nature, and from one another, turning work into a transactional and exhausting chore. [1]
•	The Economic Base and Superstructure: The economic system (the base) dictates society's legal, political, educational, and cultural institutions (the superstructure). [1, 2]
•	Inevitable Crisis and Revolution: Capitalism contains built-in economic contradictions (such as overproduction and cyclical recessions) that Marx claimed would eventually lead the working class to develop class consciousness, overthrow the system, and transition toward socialism and a classless, stateless communist society. [1, 2, 3]
Common Misconceptions (Often Addressed by Marxist Scholars)
•	Not Demanding Uniformity: Marxist theory is not about making everyone look, dress, or receive the exact same things; the goal is ending class exploitation and allowing human potential to flourish. [1]
•	Not Strictly About Violence: While Marxism does not reject revolution or view capitalism as peaceful, many theorists emphasize that systemic violence originates from existing state and economic structures rather than a desire for destruction alone. [1, 2]
