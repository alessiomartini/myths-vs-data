China maintains a highly managed financial system characterized by strict capital controls, state-directed credit allocation, and a closed-to-semi-open capital account managed by the People's Bank of China (PBOC) and the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE). [1, 2]

1. Moving Capital Abroad (Capital Controls)
China enforces a closed capital account, meaning money does not freely flow in or out of the country without regulatory approval. [1, 2]
•	Individual Quota Limit: Chinese citizens are restricted to converting and moving a maximum of US$50,000 per year in foreign currency. Transfers or investments exceeding this amount require explicit approval or permits from SAFE. [1, 2]
•	Institutional Investment Caps: Outbound institutional investment is tightly channelled through government-approved quotas (such as the Qualified Domestic Institutional Investor or QDII programs). [1]
•	Crackdowns and Scrutiny: Because of domestic economic pressures and capital flight concerns, authorities closely monitor large transactions, cash movements, and digital channels (with virtual currencies/cryptocurrencies strictly banned). Individuals attempting to evade these rules rely on informal or illicit methods like underground banks or "smurfing" (splitting funds across multiple quotas), which face aggressive enforcement. [1, 2, 3]

2. The Housing Market (From Hyper-Incentivized to Managed Correction)
•	Historical Context: For decades, real estate was heavily incentivized and viewed as the primary safe-haven asset for Chinese households, backed by local governments and state banks. [1]
•	The 2021 Collapse & Policy Shifts: Following a massive debt-driven property bubble and the contraction of major developers, the landscape shifted. Beijing has worked to de-risk the sector and re-channel domestic savings away from speculative property and into state-led technological self-reliance, domestic manufacturing, and local debt restructuring. [1, 2]
•	Current Rules: While local restrictions (such as purchase limitations) have been selectively eased or shortened in various regions to stabilize liquidity, the era of unbridled real estate speculation is largely over, and authorities discourage massive wealth migration out of the country into foreign property markets. [1, 2]

3. Other Key Financial Market Restrictions
•	Dual-Currency System (CNY vs. CNH): The Renminbi is split into the onshore yuan (CNY), which is tightly band-managed and controlled daily by the PBOC, and the offshore yuan (CNH), which trades more freely outside mainland China. [1]
•	Restricted Foreign Access: While foreign direct investment (FDI) has historically been welcomed in specific sectors, foreign portfolio inflows into domestic stock and bond markets face strict quotas, opaque approval systems, and regulatory hurdles. [1, 2]
•	State Dominance of Banking: The financial sector is anchored by major state-owned banks that funnel domestic household savings into state priorities, infrastructure, and industrial policy rather than high-yield market products for retail investors. [1]
